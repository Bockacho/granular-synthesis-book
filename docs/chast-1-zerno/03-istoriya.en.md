# Where it all came from

The idea of the grain is almost 80 years old, and it reached ordinary studios about twenty years ago. The path went from a physicist to composers, from them to university labs, and from there to your DAW.

**1946–1947. Dennis Gabor, physicist.** Gabor proposed looking at sound as a stream of tiny portions, quanta. He showed that any sound can be broken down into short bursts, each with its own frequency and its own place in time. In 1946 he also built the first granulator: a "kinematic frequency converter" based on a 16-millimeter film projector. With it Gabor changed the pitch of a sound without changing its length, and the other way round ([Roads et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1162/comj_a_00613)). Later, in 1971, Gabor would receive the Nobel Prize, though for holography.

**1950. Werner Meyer-Eppler, acoustician.** One of the founders of the electronic music studio of West German Radio (WDR) in Cologne. In August 1950, at the summer courses for new music in Darmstadt, he told composers about Gabor's quanta and kept talking about them all through the decade, including at conductor Hermann Scherchen's studio in Gravesano, where Xenakis was often a guest. That is how the idea of the grain passed from physics into music ([Roads et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1162/comj_a_00613)).

**1958–1960. Iannis Xenakis, composer.** Xenakis was the first to build music out of grains. For the piece "Analogique B" he glued together tiny pieces of magnetic tape with sine tones, arranging them by the laws of probability. There were thousands of splices, all by hand, with scissors and glue. He set out the theory in the book "Formalized Music".

**1974–1975. Curtis Roads, composer and programmer.** In the autumn of 1974 Roads wrote a series of technical studies to test Xenakis's theory on a computer. The result was an eight-minute granular study from 1975, the first in history made by a computer ([Roads, "Path to Half Life"](https://www.curtisroads.net/s/04-Path-to-Half-Life.pdf)). One such study took hours of calculation: the computer produced sound much more slowly than real time. In 1981 at MIT Roads was the first to cut recorded sound into grains, still not in real time, and spread the grains across four speakers. In 1991 Roads invented pulsar synthesis, and in 2001 he published the book "Microsound", still the main textbook on the subject.

**1986. Barry Truax, composer.** Truax was the first to make grains sound in real time: on a DMX-1000 signal processor at Simon Fraser University in Vancouver. You turn a knob and the sound changes at once. His piece "Riverrun" was made on this system ([Truax, Granular Synthesis](https://www.sfu.ca/~truax/gran.html)). Truax's programs were called GSX and GSAMX. They already had what later became the basis of every granulator: access to the parameters of each grain, presets and ramps, that is, smooth movement of parameters at a set speed. Any parameter could be changed with a single keystroke right while the sound played. A separate program, GRMSKX, added tendency masks. A tendency mask sets the limits within which a parameter is chosen at random, and these limits themselves move smoothly in time. A mask could be built as a smooth transition from one preset to another, and Truax improvised by hand on top of the prepared material ([Truax, 1988](https://doi.org/10.2307/3679938)). The grains of "Riverrun" were built from simple waves and FM signals. The system could also cut recorded sound into grains, but the processor's memory held only 150–170 ms of sound. This tiny buffer could be filled continuously by a live input, so the first live-sound granulator worked with a stretch of a sixth of a second.

**1990s. Labs and modular environments.** Granular instruments live at IRCAM in Paris, at GRM, in Csound and Max. Mostly academic composers know how to use them. Native Instruments Reaktor brings granular synthesis to everyday producers: ensembles such as Grainstates and Travelizer appear in it, where the position in a sample can be moved in real time like a filter knob.

**2010s. A granulator in every DAW.** In 2012 Robert Henke, co-founder of Monolake, releases Granulator II for Max for Live, and every owner of Ableton Live Suite gets access to granular synthesis. Apple buys Camel Audio and builds its Alchemy synthesizer, with a granular engine, into Logic Pro. Granular modes appear in big software synths, and in hardware as well: on modules such as Mutable Instruments Clouds and Make Noise Morphagene.

**Today.** Granular synthesis has become a standard feature. It is in Serum 2, in Pigments, in the third version of Granulator. What Xenakis spent months on with scissors is now done with one knob in a second.

??? info "Under the hood"

    Gabor relied on the same uncertainty principle as quantum mechanics: the shorter a sound event, the wider its spectrum, and the other way round. You can't know both the moment and the frequency of a sound exactly. For a granulator this is a direct consequence: a short grain always sounds wider and noisier than a long one, even if it is cut from a pure tone.
